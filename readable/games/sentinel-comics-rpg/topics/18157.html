I think the issue with the Laser Dragon came from two separate, but linked points:

1) The communication that the LD was all but impervious to attack.  That's the sort of thing that has to be established from the very outset; when a character unleashes an attack against it, a GM has to be explicit in stating--both in and out of character--that the attack isn't going to have any effect, no matter how well the character rolls.  That can be described in any number of ways, but also should be accompanied by the out-of-character statement that, "Your normal attacks aren't going to do the trick here; try to find something else..."

2) The lack of ultimate goal of what to 'do' with the LD, once it was established that the dragon couldn't be harmed.  Was the goal to drive it into space?  Get it back into the egg?  Immobilize it?  Send it to another dimension?  This is something the players should probably have taken the lead on, but some GM direction, "Okay, so attacking it isn't getting you anywhere.  What's your next plan?" could have really gone a long way towards player organization and strategy.

 

One thing I'm *not* a fan of is the ability to "game" the system, by passing the initiative order from GM -- Scene -- GM -- Scene.  That seems really cheesy to me, and takes away a good deal of player agency.  I'm likely to already consider enforcing the rule of "you can't pass to yourself" a little harsher, with the scene only ever advancing after every other actor has gone and, once it's gone, must be passed to someone other than the GM.

So, I've been putting a lot of thought into the initiative system.  At first, I thought it was clever, different, and simple, but now I'm not so sure.

First, the Sentinels RPG is, overall, tactics-light.  Mostly, when your turn comes up, you look at the situation and do what you think your character might see as the most pressing issue that's within their skillset to address.  That's what the players in this game did, and it's what I see people do in the starter kit games I've seen demoed.  But choosing turn order is a surprisingly difficult and tactics-rich decision.  Take all your turns first so you can eliminate threats and defend yourselves, but let the villains take double turns?  Let the villains go early so you can chain a bunch of boosts and coordinated attacks?  Use an ability that lasts until the end of your next turn, which discourages you from acting early in the next round?

I don't mind tactical decisions--I love tactical games in general--but that's not the strength of the Sentinels RPG.  It's a smooth, simple system with a focus on storytelling and roleplay.  I don't feel like the initiative system is a good fit.  Notice how, in the live game, the only time the players stopped to discuss strategy with each other was when deciding turn order.  They could have discussed it at other times (to give each other boosts, defend each other, etc.), but after the first couple of turns, it was the turn order that derailed them from organic play into tactical play.

Second, turn order isn't a character decision, it's a player decision.  This is the bigger problem, I think.  When you decide who goes next, it's you making that decision, not your character.  Notice how in the live game, the players only stopped thinking from their characters' viewpoints when the decision of turn order came up.  I felt like it significantly broke the flow and immersion.

That's even weirder when considering the GM's perspective.  A villain shouldn't have any concept of taking turns--that's a game mechanic, not an in-world thing.  The villain isn't the one deciding who should go first, the GM is.  Should the GM think strategically in favor of the villain just because it's their turn?  What if the villain is a group of mindless minions--should they make inferior decisions?  And when the GM is taking the environment's/scene tracker's turn, their decision can only be based purely on narrative at best, antagonism against the heroes at worst.  I feel like this is actually harder than GM'ing D&D--in D&D, you just have to decide what the monster you're playing as would do, not what "the story" would do.  The GM has to deliberately decide to make things easier or harder for the players--which, yeah, is part of a GM's job, but it's an extra level of challenge that doesn't seem necessary.

So yeah, I'm thinking a more traditional initiative method might serve this whole system better.

That's certainly an interesting analysis, Mindwanderer.  I could definitely see why some groups would want to drop the initiative system (either for more in-character time* or faster play).  The biggest problem I've had running the starter kit is players taking "too long" to dedide what they want to do on their turn, and that has been solved by having players say who they'll pass to at the start of their own turn, so that the next person can start thinking about what they want to do earlier.  If I were to drop the system, I'd probably adopt the order set by the card game for simplicity.

*Interestingly, the starter kit provides an alternative way to think about a game of the RPG: it suggests that the players/GM are a group of comic-book writers putting together a story, with the GM acting as editor-in-chief.  Now, pretending to be a guy writing about Absolute Zero seems to me FAR more boring than pretending to be Absolute Zero, so I tossed this suggestion aside in less time than it took to read it.  But if you were to play the game imagining that you're the writing staff, the current initiative system is perfectly in-character.  "Oh! It would be so cool if Abs. Zero were to go next, he could do [this]!"

As to the episode itself, I didn't manage to listen to the whole thing (I started the episode too close to bedtime to watch the whole thing), but the second that Christopher put overcome tickboxes on the relic-things it was obvious to me that the goal of the scene was to deal with them and not the dragon.  I'm surprised to read that they didn't until closer to the end of scene.  (That said, just because it was obvious to me doesn't mean it should be to everyone).
