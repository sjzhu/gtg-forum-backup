Sentinels RPG is a comic book role-playing game, intended to tell a fun comic book story. It’s not “a day in the life of a superhero”. Silver age comic books are short, sweet, and to the point. While you certainly could just give the players free reign to determine their objectives and their approach, it leads to long, drawn out sessions where the progress of the game is entirely in the hands of the players. Anyone with gamemaster experience knows how that will go; a lot of floundering around and putzing about until a bad guy is thrown in front of them, and then they punch the bad guy until it doesn’t get up again, and they steal the bad guy’s stuff.

Occasionally you’ll have a group that can stay on task, but those seem to be the exception and not the norm.

Railroad-ey? Yeah, I can see that. But in this system, that’s kind of the point. You’re not really trying to role-play a hero so much as you’re role-playing a group of writers for a comic book, role-playing as heroes in a brainstorming session to create an exciting comic book story. And to do that, the editor-in-chief (gamemaster) needs to keep the writers (players) on task.

Ultimately all a gamemaster for any role-playing game is doing is determining an end goal and putting up obstacles on the way to that end goal. Every session is going to have railroad tracks laid down, it’s just a matter of how good the gamemaster is at covering those tracks and making the players think they’re in control of the direction of the story. They really aren’t unless you put them in a sandbox world and let them run wild.

It’s been my experience, though, that while players say they want the freedom to do anything they want, when they actually have such freedom, they don’t know what to do with it. It’s overwhelming to them. They would rather have the gamemaster drop the hook right in their mouths but not look like they’re dropping the hook right in their mouths. They want to be taken for a ride but not look like the gamemaster is doing the driving. It’s all one big illusion. Lies upon lies, as the creators say.

Laying out the objectives in front of the players and then starting a timer gets players moving. It lights a fire under their butts and Spurs them to take action. How they tackle the objectives is what makes them a hero. If they walk away or say, Naw, that’s too railroad-ey," and want to do something else, then the action of the scene continues without them while their hero goes to a hotel and sits down for dinner. That’s not really exciting comic book story, and if that were to happen at my table then I pull that player aside and ask them why they are there. What do they hope to accomplish by not playing the game? Because if a player does that, then it’s a big sign to me that they don’t want to play or don’t like the game. If they aren’t interested, then that’s a waste of time and effort not just on their part, but on my part and the part of all the other players who are there who want to play. I have no problem cutting that player loose, because honestly, I have enough people around who want to play at my table that should one leave another will take their place. I’m lucky to have that option available to me.

I don’t think this is railroading at all, personally. I think it’s how the game is meant to be played. Superheroes need obstacles and objectives, at least when starting out. After they get the hang of how to be a hero, you could slowly transfer identifying objectives over to them, but that’s a learning process and not a first time playing strategy.

The end goal of the Sentinels RPG is to tell an exciting superhero comic book story. I think a gamemaster should keep that in mind constantly when running it. Superhero comic books don’t have pages or panels or ink to waste waiting around for the heroes to act. Readers don’t have time to read through four issues before something exciting happens. And it has been made clear that Sentinels is a Silver Age comic series, so there isn’t a lot of gritty realism or grey area dilemmas to ponder. It’s evil doers afoot and terrible plots that don’t make sense in the real world but for a comic book story it works. They’re exploring crossover stories and metaplots that span multiple issues and space travel that happens without real world science.

It seems that each session of Sentinels needs a sense of urgency to it. I can tell you from personal player experience that the index card element adds that sense of urgency and overwhelming odds while also keeping it clear what needs to be done to save the day. Don’t knock it until you try it.

I do think that action scenes in a Sentinels RPG session need to feel high stakes action that is worthy of the attention of a superhero, particularly since that seems to be the point of action scenes in the Sentinels RPG. Whereas montage scenes are the exposition, the all-roleplay downtime between the times when the city is in peril. One entire session of Sentinels RPG is supposed to be one comic book issue, so a montage scene that eats up all of one session should probably have some really juicy character development or plot advancement to be worthy of “publishing” so to speak.

There’s ways to work that kind of thing. Tachyon goes to a fancy restaurant expecting to meet with her wife, but then she gets there and finds herself sitting across from Friction, her nemesis! If Tachyon can get through this dinner without creating a scene and just hears Friction out, then Friction will leave and all the bombs will be disarmed ten minutes after Friction exits the restaurant.

Now we have a montage scene that engages the superhero in role-play with stakes (steaks?) that makes it not an ordinary dinner scene. There’s a purpose to it, one that likely will lead to the next scene of action as Tachyon needs to disarm the bombs and catch Friction, or maybe Spite is hunting Friction for reasons and she wants Tachyon’s help, or maybe Friction knows that Biomancer is doing terrible things that involve replacing her, or any number of other comic book plot tropes that explains why Friction needs to have dinner with Tachyon. Maybe she just wants to have dinner with her. I don’t know. But it should be viewed through the lens of what would happen in a comic book; of what would engage a comic book reader.

That’s the experience that I think the Sentinels RPG is trying to provide. I’m not saying that other experiences are bad or that they’re wrong or that I hate them. I’m saying that the purpose of the game is to do what heroes do. Defend the weak and the innocent. Throw a wrench in the works when the villains set their plans in motion. Jump in and lend a hand when your teammates are in trouble. Stand up for your principles. Punch evil right in the face.

Save the day.

After all, that’s what it says right in the Gameplay Guide.

And if you think there’s no role-playing during an action scene, honey, you have no idea. There is a ton of role-playing. Especially in this system since you have to role-play it first, then put together the dice. In other systems it’s usually the other way around, where the rules tell you what to do and the role-play is secondary to the roll-play during combat.

One thing to keep reminding your players if they feel under pressure from the scene tracker, is that they can use an overcome to prevent it advancing in the scene's turn, of course a critical fail (0 or less) could result in it advancing further.

So in the scenes green zone you've just arrived on the scene and are still taking things in before realising how bad things are.

The Yellow is the majority of the actual conflict, the pressure is on and you're attempting to resolve things before it gets worse.

In Red things are really bad, the building is groaning really loudly and the supports holding it up are starting to snap, the hostage taker is aiming his weapon at the hostages, the firing sequence for the Terra-Luna Impulsion Device has starting counting down to the actual firing time and it's time to pull out all the stops.

This serves two purposes,

1. It gives the players a representation of how bad things are getting adding an element of tension for the players.

2. I means that when the chips are down, people are not being hamstrung by taking too little damage to help effectively if the abilities they need are in the red or yellow zone.

My point throughout this argument is no more and no less than this:  in an action scene, using hard deadlines both in-character (perhaps through the GYRO scene tracker) and out of character (through visual, verbal, and other social cues) maintains and enhances the tension of that scene.  If you take away that pressure, you also remove the tension from the scene.

As an overarching game-design philosophy, Cold Steel Wardens was designed to articulate and give voice to the moral quandary found in the Iron Age of Comics.  In the heat of the moment of an action scene, bullets fly, swords swing, punches land, and blood spills.  Yes, the chance to examine that quandary exists, but not in the depth or capacity of what the SCRPG would call a montage scene or in open-roleplay.  The depth emerges when the consequences are finally revealed:  when a vigilante sees where the bullets landed or when a would-be hero arrives just a little too late.  Without that tension, the whole game easily becomes meaningless; it's not something I'd ever be willing to sacrifice.  For me, that means that when running the SCRPG, I'm going to keep the GYRO scene tracker open, visible, and oft-utilized during action scenes.  During montage or open-roleplay scenes, I won't use it.  No more, no less.
