I highly doubt that they’d stop selling the Sentinels core game, so it will still be making them money. Heck, if they simply re-issued what they have with errata changes and new art bells and whistles, they’d inject themselves right onto store shelves again and lots of current owners would re-buy everything.

From their perspective, they may be looking at how much money Sentinels makes over time, whether expansions are selling progressively more or less than previous ones, and how well the base game does after all this time. They are probably also looking at how to get their audience to grow, not just how to keep the one they have. Put another way: Dominion expansions are still coming out…but are we buying them as much any more? I’m not.

Another thing that doesn’t necessarily go away with the end of Sentinels’ story is the game mechanic. There’s absolutely no reason why they can’t repurpose or modify the mechanic for new games. Get a posse to go fight outlaws in the old west? Go questing through a dungeon? Overthrow a tyrannical space empire? Board and pirate merchantmen on the high seas? All could easily fit into the hero/villain/environment deck structure. And all could easily have as big a pile of lore as Sentinels.

I bet, in the end, Christopher and Adam may just want to try some new things. They may feel like additional Sentinels expansions are going to come out more and more gimmicky in an effort to be distinct from what already exists. They might have some other ideas they want to devote their time to.

I share your apprehension a little, as I haven’t seen >G penetrate into several of my local game stores and I hope they can put out new things as high-caliber as Sentinels. I’m looking forward to Spirit Island, but that will be something entirely new - which comes with its own apprehension. In the end the reason I’m most sad to see the end of the Sentinels story is just that I like it and I’m sorry to see it go. But it’s always going to be here on my game shelf, and those boxes are filled with a LOT of combinations. If it’s going to end, due to whatever business case factors that I can’t see, I’d rather it do so in a planned, graceful way. And I’d much rather a planned ending than a continuation as a tent-pole property that becomes more and more likely to included big misses as it does hits (probably none of which will be as much a hit as the original). Down that road lies Jar-Jar Binks. Let’s not wish the same fate on Thiago!

Look at what happened with the ARG. If members of this community were just interested in gameplay, the ARG would have fallen flat.

For many of us, I think, part of the charm of SotM is that not only had it a planned storyline, but there were clues to future characters and events all over the cards. You look at your cards,while playing and ask yourself : who is this villain they are fighting ? Whose red boot is it on this card ? This flavor text seems to allude to something, but what ?

What will happen next time ? Who will be the new heroes and villains ? Will we learn something new about already known characters ? This is not "story" per se - but the slow reveal of the multiverse and all the clues and foreshadowing in the game itself that kept many of us hooked to the game far more than the gameplay itself could. Like a good TV show, you come back because you want to know what happens next, you want to confirm the theory you built seeing the first few episodes - you want to see the resolution of all these little mysteries. And like a good (or bad!) TV show, we have a full forum of fan theories. And Fan art. And fan fiction.

So, yes, the gameplay is good - but I don't think the game would have had such success - and following - if it did not have this "planned from the start" facet. We humans like to discover that things were not completely random. Give us a basegame where a lot of cards include characters we don't know, and we will impatiently wait for the reveal of who they are and what they are doing here

However, like any good TV show, it can't go on forever. There has to be a complete reveal, and sooner than later, or you lose interest. Mysteries are meant to be solved. The alien conspiracy has to be finally revealed and understood, or the X-Files will close because of lack of viewers. The Winter has to come - and go - or bad, bad things will happen to you.

I think that, if GtG hadn't "planned"* this whole story arc that is now ending, and hadn't sprinkled the base game and each expansion with breadcumbs alluding to what would happen later, the game would not have been as successful. Or, rather, the community would not have been as faithful.

And, you now what ? There are still breadcrumbs that probably won't be solved in OblivAeon…

OK, since this is all a thought experiment anayway, here's a question:Let's say that instead of making the big this-is-the-end announcement (which we all pretty much knew this was anyhow), what if they had said something like: We changed our minds. We're going to do a new 2-hero/2-villain/2-4 environment expansion every 12-18 months, and after every 5th expansion, we'll do a Vengeance/Villains-style expansion to bring those heroes into that mechanic. How long would you keep buying expansions? Related question: How many people do you think would pick up the game knowing there were 10 or 15 or 25 expansions to buy?

I first discovered SotM around the time of the Shattered Timelines release, and really liked playing the game, but I only considered actually buying into it after I learned about the limited number of expansions planned, and the world's closed story arc. This, to me, is essential to the game's appeal.

I like playing MtG, and some of the Fantasy Flight LCG's, but I could never commit to buying any cards for those games because 1) they're goddamn money sinks (especially for an obsessisive completionist like myself), and 2) the constant release of expansions make so many of the cards and mechanics feel ephemeral to me, and the worldbuilding diffuse and bland. There's so much personality and detail in SotM, and a true sense of narrative and character in the game's universe, which is something I haven't found in any other card/board game (that aren't RPG's)—and I don't think this can be achieved without setting an endpoint.

Whether that's the best business decision, I'm not sure. But I personally would never have bought a single card had the plan been to release expansions indefinitely, or for a much longer period of time—and now I'm an obsessive fan.

I think GtG has shown courage and integrity by commiting to this risky plan, focusing on the quality of this game rather than its longevity, and investing in new prospects and assets (and perhaps revisiting some older ones) as the company hopefully grows.
