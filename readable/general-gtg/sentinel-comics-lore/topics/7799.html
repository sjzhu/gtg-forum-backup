Nielzabub:

Except that the point of the Lovecraftiverse is that no amount of power is ever enough.  Indeed, pretty much Lovecraft's entire concept was that the more science learns about the world, the more hopeless humanity's place in the world becomes - which is at best questionably true for other reasons, but the whole reason he invented Cthulhu was to personify his feelings on the topic, and so Cthulhu represents the ultimate "shut up you're wrong" to any arguments against the original premise he's connected to, which was in large part that there are some fights you can't possibly win, riddles you can't solve and would be happier not trying to.  Superman, in particular (being the original and archetypal superhero, and the invention of a couple of Jewish kids who needed a symbol of hopefulness to escape the troubles of their lives), is likewise a "shut up you're wrong" in favor of a different premise - he brings godlike, unstoppable power to the cause of Truth, Justice and the American Way, while Cthulhu brings ultimate, inescapable horror to the cause of Humanity is Doomed And Nothing Can Save Us.  If you disagree with either of those premises, that's perfectly fine, it just means you really shouldn't use the character who exists only to prove them right (or other very similar characters, and there's no way Ixthulhu's name is a coincidence…if you want a horrible-but-ultimately-beatable alien god-thing, you shouldn't try to conflate him with an unbeatable one).

You can't say that mixing the two mythos don't work when the story produced by it is actually entertaining and fun to watch.

I can and do say exactly that (with the understanding that I am speaking from partial ignorance, and might change my mind upon actually watching the whole thing).  Sometimes, the point of a story is not just to be entertaining and fun to watch (indeed, many of the greatest and most beloved stories are utterly gut-wrenching tragedies and rage-inducers…Romeo & Juliet and A Game of Thrones spring to mind offhand).  Sometimes, it exists to deliver an Aesop, and anything which breaks the Aesop can utterly destroy the raison d'etre of the story…or, in this case, a story element which was used in a story where it didn't belong.

That's like saying you can't make a good comedy with zombies because the existence of zombies is diametrically opposite to the tone of a comedy, and putting them in a comedy is a huge mistreatment of them. Both Fido and Shawn of the Dead are fantastic movies. The zombies are the same as they've always been in both of those movies, but the protagonists treat them differently. It's a different kind of story than what those characters are normally in, but simply changing genres does not invalidate or ruin a character. It just shows them in a different light.

Sometimes, showing something in a different light exposes hideous flaws which scar all perceptions of its worth altogether.  This is the entire reason that fashion photos are airbrushed…one unflattering image becomes a blight upon the career of someone who was once valued for their apparent flawlessness.  If you turn Cthulhu into a laughingstock, then people who encounter him first as a laughingstock will never appreciate what he should be.  If someone wrote a Superman story in which he chain-smoked and beat his wife, DC Comics would sue the pants off that person, because they aren't just using DC's "intellectual property" without permission, but horribly misusing it, in a way that irreparably damages their brand…people would see that story and think that was the "real" Superman.  Unfortunately, nobody owns Cthulhu, and thus nobody can stop writers from abusing Cthulhu in exactly such a fashion.  (I think IP law is an imperfect solution at best for preventing such mis-representations of characters, but it's the one we have at the moment.)

In conclusion, if Cthulhu (or his thinly-disguised pastiche) is to appear in a superhero story at all, I want him to be treated with the gravity he deserves.  Consider the way X-Men comics handled the Legacy Virus…it was a metaphor for AIDS, and because AIDS hadn't been cured, the writers weren't allowed to cure it, but had to write about how the heroes handled the fact that they were victims of something they couldn't win against.  That's what you use Cthulhu for - to show how your characters deal with loss, futility, and ultimate doom.  Whether superheroes ever should accept the inevitability of their defeat, when doing exactly the opposite is pretty much their raison d'etre, is another question (and I don't think it's a coincidence that Marvel, not DC, did the Legacy Virus).  But that brings me right back to my "Cthulhu should probably not be in superheroes" premise.

Psychopathy is essentially a subset of sociopathy. That is, psychopathy is associated with decreased (or absent) levels of empathy, high anti-social tendencies, etc, while sociopathy is any disorder that may lead to social dysfunction. A sociopath is much broader :D
