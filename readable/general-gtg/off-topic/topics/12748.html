I've GMed a lot of campaigns, and this is the first time I haven't had (at least) two parallel games running at the same time in years. We're currently playing a (simplified) Traveller game (Mongoose's rule set, because they are the lightest of the latest versions).

I’m also planning a sequel to a customized-rules campaign that used Everway as the basis but puts a little more structure around it to allow some better management of character capabilities and expectations. Our customization is based on something called Amberway. (If you like Everway, you might appreciate it.)

Last year we wrapped up two campaigns:

  • A Pathfinder test to see how we could simplify and streamline it (as some of our players prefer lighter rules structures), using the Council of Thieves adventure path from Paizo. The fact the players get to actually become "actors" in a play as part of the adventure path was the key reason I chose that one - I knew some of the folks we play with would really enjoy that!
  • A long-running (16-year) D&D 2.7 (homebrewed D&D 2nd edition with the some of the Player's Options rules included) game that I'd found myself frustrated with from a rules perspective. It was set in an old campaign setting I'd created a long time ago that started as AD&D, upgraded to AD&D 2nd, then evolved to the customized version we were playing over the years. I'm hoping to start another campaign set there in a couple years, but using a new ruleset (probably either a lighter version of Pathfinder or D&D 5th, depending on the rules available at the time, the players involved, etc.).
If you've ever heard of/read Robin's Laws of Good Game Mastering (and if you're a GM, I hope you have - it's a simple and useful little book), I'm primarily a Tactician and a Storyteller, which is an odd but interesting combo when I play. Having said that, I very rarely play, as I am usually GMing. :sunglasses: (And if you know about the book, there's a simple, online quiz for looking at your types. If you don't know the book, you're welcome to take the quiz but it might not make a lot of sense without knowing what those types mean and how they play out.)

For a really, really simple system you can use Dread. It is usually used as a one-shot sessions instead of a campaign. Character creation is based on answering a questions about your character, instead of creating stats and numbers (which aren't in the game). Instead of using dice to determine if you succeed or fail, you have a Jenga tower. Depending on how difficult the task is, the GM determines how many blocks you pull. If the tower falls, you character is out of the game. That's it.

Welcome back. Sorry to hear about the problems. Glad you're getting better.

I like rules light systems (though I admit I also enjoy rolling dice and having SUPER huge bonuses to things, whether it's a +12 sword or the ability to make just about any knowledge role). 

Rabit is often my GM and he, I, and the people we generally game with are very focused when starting a campaign on identifying what everyone is looking for out of the campaign and crafting something we'll all enjoy. Then he goes off and does a ton of work that he purports to enjoy in order to provide a setting and set of rules that fit what we're looking for. We do our part in character creation and we all work together during game play. It's pretty great, really. I'm blessed. 

For our current campaign we went through our most extensive discussions yet before settling on the game and the characters. We spend way too much time on our game for it not to be fun. These included picking a genre (Sci Fi), considering half a dozen or so characteristics of gaming (level of silliness, amount of fighting, number of expected sessions, etc.), picking a subgenre (Firefly-like crew of a transport), and then jointly brainstorming the types of characters/skills that might be in such a party and staking out mostly non-overlapping characters. From there Rabit did world building and the rest of us went into character development, with each of us working w/ Rabit and sometimes with each other to craft backgrounds that would fit with each other and the world.

The resulting game play can look pretty banal to others. We often have very little action and progress the plot quite slowly. We played last night, for instance, and I think our entire session consisted of discussions with each other and a dinner meeting with a client. But think about it. What did you like about Firefly? Was it the fights? For me, it was how the characters interacted. In the parlance of Robin's Laws, most of our group are Storytellers and Method Actors, though much of the other types are also represented. I am primarly a Method Actor and a Power Gamer. My characters all tend to be interested in being powerful, but they are interested in all sorts of different power for all sorts of different reasons. My character in this campaign has virtually no fighting skills, but is extremely well connected. In most situations, he's the person that can get the crew in the door -- get somebody to hear our side, take a meeting, give information, or lend resources.

I'm a long-time roleplayer, which is how I got into board games in the first place.  I started with D&D 2e (the black books with the red borders), back when I was in middle school.  Even before that, I was addicted to the Choose Your Own Adventure books and Wizards, Warriors, and You.  

When I was in undergrad, I founded the Wittenberg Role-Playing Guild.  That group's lasted for 13 years and just held their 12th consecutive convention.  The WRPG's been a busy group in the past few  years, with a good many of us entering the gaming industry.  I founded Blackfall Press and wrote/produced Cold Steel Wardens: Roleplaying in the Iron Age of Comics.  In addition to that, I freelance for several other companies, including Cubicle 7 Entertainment, GameWick Games, Gun Metal Games, and a handful of others.

Eric Ebbs (who joined two years after the guild was founded) kickstarted and created Era of the Ninja, which he'll be selling at GenCon.  Will Herrmann (another former Guild President), a huge Savage Worlds fan, created the framework for his Wild Card Creator as a senior computer science thesis project, which he's turned into the smoothest, most intuitive SW character generation software out there.  Katrina Ostrander (a third Guild President) currently works for Fantasy Flight Games--she produced several of the current Star Wars roleplaying books, but now works in FFG's fiction department.

My current home group has a wide variety of tastes.  Right now, we're playing the Savage Worlds: Hell on Earth plot point campaign, "The Worm's Turn".  Before that, they were playtesting some material for my CSW follow-up book.  

Other systems I enjoy?  Lady Blackhawk is a great little micro-system, and the creator has a phenomenal game up on KS right now (Blades in the Dark).  Cartoon Action Hour and ICONS are phenomenal games.  I'm itching to run a game of All for One: Regime Diabolique, but I'm not sure when that's going to happen.

You can run pretty much any tabletop RPG on a forum, if RPOL (Roleplay Online) is anything to go by…DnD is one of the most popular (that and Pathfinder) in fact. I've also played in a couple of DnD games on an entirely different forum before I joined RPOL (the guy who ran that first DnD game was the one who ended up pointing me at RPOL when someone he knew on there was starting up a game of Mouse Guard and he knew I'd mentioned being interested in it at some point). Really, you just need a bunch of threads (OOC thread, IC thread, maybe a rules thread, stuff like that) and it's helpful if there's also somewhere the GM can store their own resources (though they can just use their own hard drive, I suppose ;)). Having character sheets visible to the GM helps a lot, too. Oh, and in terms of actual gameplay, finding a way to tweak combat so that one round doesn't take about three weeks of real time is kind of useful too ;).
