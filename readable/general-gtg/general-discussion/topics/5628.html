A retrovirus works by injecting its DNA into the cells of its host. Each time the host cell replicates, the DNA of the virus is replicated as well.

Retroviral gene therapy works by taking a retrovirus and removing its DNA and replacing it with the DNA you want added to the host cells. The retrovirus then gets it into most or all of the host cells, and the change is permanent (in theory) since the host cells copy the genes each time they divide. A retrovirus is not the only way to introduce new DNA to a host body, but it is one of the more versatile, efficient, and cheap ways to introduce a wide variety genes (other treatments often require much more customization).

In the real world, retroviral gene therapy is being researched as a way to cure many genetic conditions. Inherited diseases such as Huntington’s disease, Parkinson’s disease and many immune system disorders and even hereditary vision problems like color blindness are excellent candidates for gene therapy treatment. It may even be possible in the future for specialized genes to be used to treat non-hereditary diseases. For example: sharks have a series of genes that effectively prevent most forms of tumor. This gene could be modified and inserted into humans (actually, we have tumor suppression genes of our own, they would just have to be made more effective), to make humans immune to cancer. This is still a long way away, if it is ever possible at all, but the theory is sound. In one experiment in 2007/2008 in Germany, a man was successfully cured of HIV by killing of his bone marrow and replacing it with genetically modified bone marrow that produced cells to suppress and kill the HIV virus.

The problem with real-world gene therapy lies in its impermanence. It is virtually impossible to guarantee that the virus successfully injects the new DNA into every cell (your immune system will kill some virus off, etc.), and so the “modified” cells often become depleted and die off, making it necessary to have another round of gene therapy. Current gene therapy treatments for SCID (a genetic immune system disorder often called “bubble boy disease” because it requires the patient be completely isolated) last about 4 years before new treatments are needed.
