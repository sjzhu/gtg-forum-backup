Thusfar, the only real concerns that I have surround the Scene Tracker and its relationship with Initiative.  It just feels like too much of a meta-game to me, with the game actively punishing players for wanting to go before the villains.  In fact, doing so outright penalizes players for taking a "surprise round" against villains.

To me, that's an easy fix:

1) The environment/scene tracker always goes last in initiative order.  

2) The environment does not get to 'choose' who goes next; the last player (or GM) to take a turn chooses who starts the next round (following the Environment/Scene Tracker).

3) The GM may not pass initiative to themselves, even if they have multiple actors within the scene, unless all players have already gone.

4) Any player who chooses to pass initiative to the GM may, on their next turn, reroll a single die of their choice.

Yes, this doesn't "eliminate" the possibility of the players all going before the villain, but it does disincentivize doing so, as a player can reap a tangible, in-game benefit by passing play to the GM.  Further, it makes GM tracking easier, by keeping the scene tracker's place in initiative static.

My opinions haven't really changed, although they've become more refined in some ways.

  • Most of my concerns were about the adventure modules in the starter kit, not the core rules.  Obviously, nothing has changed my mind about those.
  • Challenges that are set up as one or more Overcome checks that need to be made really need to be spelled out, or else players won't bite on them.  Ones that are required need to be hand-fed to the players (e.g. written on index cards right off the bat).  Optional ones can be hinted at or "gated" behind other events or attempts, but the GM needs to assume that they won't go anywhere, and balance challenges appropriately.
  • Challenges are still pretty railroad-y.  The GM decides what needs to be done, the players figure out some way to justify their Powers and Qualities (and ideally a Principle) to accomplish it.  Sometimes that can result in some cool storytelling, but other times it was really strained.
  • If you want to design an effective character, the single most important thing is to choose two Principles that let you use your Max die in the widest possible range of circumstances.  Ones that require specific circumstances (Cosmic), that are hard to justify (Time Traveler, at least when you're from the past), or that don't let you use your Max die (Speed) make it so that you'll rack up a lot more failures and twists, and miss opportunities for Hero Points.  Choosing your other abilities wisely helps (especially mass attacks and persistent boosts/hinders), but the Principles are critical.
  • Christopher made a big point out of playing by the rules (except for the mecha battle and the cheer boosts), but he did exploit the vagueness of the rules extensively.  The twists he came up with were frequently much more or much less powerful than the recommended ones in the book.  A GM that's just a little off in their judgment could make adventures much harder or much easier than intended.
  • Regarding initiative: the players got fatigued.  Early on, they tried to choose their turn order very carefully to avoid negative consequences.  Later, they started making their choices almost at random.  They completely gave up on using abilities that lasted until their next turn, and never bothered setting up combos with boosts and hinders.  Christopher stopped exploiting every mistake they made, too, probably because he didn't want to punish them for it anymore (like he did in the first couple of games).  So I don't feel like the system ended up working well.
  • The system is really straightforward, but it's still fiddly for inexperienced players.  Maggie and Rae forgot about their inherent abilities and reactions nearly every time they would have been relevant.  Rae took basically the whole game to understand the difference between abilities and powers/qualities.  They all invoked their hero points and collected trades only rarely.  Having taught D&D to new players, especially 4th edition, I feel like there's a gap here--D&D is overall a much more complicated game, but it's less error-prone to get started with.
  • The Scene Tracker is indeed iffy sometimes.  The villain being able to accelerate it is particularly problematic--when that happens, the players run a real risk of losing due to it.  A villain that really "wants to win" will be better off accelerating the scene tracker than trying to damage the heroes.  Of course, the system is designed such that the GM really can't go all-out trying to "beat" the players, otherwise they'll just have a bunch of minions gang up on one hero.
  • The game isn't about "progression."  It just isn't, get over it.  On the plus side, that means it's well-suited for shorter series and one-shots.  I think people who want to play campaigns of 20+ sessions will start feeling bored with their characters, but then, the superhero genre really isn't about that.  I think Critical Hits could design a spinoff system in a fantasy/sci-fi setting that used the core Sentinels system and build a new game with leveled progression, but that would be something else entirely.

Thoughts and notes after watching the character creation session and Issue #1.  I will follow up with more as I go.

Character creation uses random die rolls.  I am kind of curious what the purpose of this is.  It seemed a little bit tacked on, maybe to satisfy people who just want random dice rolls because reasons.  Maybe useful for generating ideas if you really just don't know.  Maybe someone on the design team has some deeper insights here though.  I know games I like have seemingly weird stuff in character creation that actually works nicely in practice (Name lists in dungeon world, for example).  

Character creation might be a little bit lenghty.  It might be something that can be done individually outside of an actual play session though, at least in part, which can mitigate this.  This is not a concern, just an observation.

 

A lot of what you get when listening to a charismatic storyteller like Christopher is going to have nothing to do with the game itself, and everything to do with the person running the game.  So it requires some careful attention to separate out the two parts of this, and without the rules in front of me to reference, I still can't fully differentiate between what is the GM and what is the rules.  So everything has to be taken with a grain of salt.

At 26 minutes in to the first issue, Christopher asks Craig to make an overcome roll to investigate an energy signature he detects.  Its not entirely clear what the point of having him role here is.  Is there a specific rule that says when a roll is triggered, or is it just GM discretion?  Is the GM just using this as a generic "investigate roll", with all the problems that come with that?  What would have happened if he had failed the investigation roll?  My guess is that things would have worked out almost identically, because saying "You find nothing, I guess we stop playing" doesn't work for good story telling.  As it stands, his "success" did not seem very valuable.  All he got was "Thats sure some energy signature there.  Lots of magic".  Which isn't useful for him beyond saying "Go talk to your teammate" which is a given regardless of the investigation result.  Even if he hadn't been given the detail that it was magical, one of the other 3 player would have been given that detail, since they had even more ability to understand magic than he did.  

Another overcome role is made at the 54 minute mark.  This one the GM rules that one of the players principles can't be used, but when the player fails, the "major twist" is just that the previously overruled principle kicks in and gives the information anyways.  Again, what are the actual rules on overcomes and investigations?  Do the rules explicitly tell GMs not to do these time wasting behaviors, and the GM here just didn't care, or do they not give any meaningful guidance at all?  Do the rules actually encourage this self defeating behavior?  This one isn't quite as bad as the first one I mentioned.  Its actually possible for the adventure to continue here without successful knowledge checks.

This next one is less of a complaint about the game, and more a complaint about the way designers and fans have inaccurately described the game.  There are multiple points where mechanical decisions are made before the fiction is consulted, sometimes without even bothering to check the fiction.  Two examples:  Starblight gets chomped by a dragon and the GM informs the player that his next turn can only be an overcome action.  Later, Violet Heart uses a rage ribbons attack against some minions.  The attack misses, meaning the minions are not destroyed but they are reduced a die size.  No one ever says what this means in the fiction though.  This is probably fine.  It might be tedious, in this particular system, to always go back to the fiction.  But the fact that this is even possible shows that the game is fundamentally incompatible with PbtA design principles.  So its frustrating that so many people, including the actual designers of the game, have claimed Apocalypse World is a source for the design, when it absolutely is not, at least in the presented materials we have.

Some of my views of this system:

 

The Scene Tracker: I like the concept as it allows more rapid access to certain abilities without requiring you to take damage and builds tension. I feel the trick to the tracker is more in how it is approached. I feel people view it as a lose scenario while I treat it more as a linker between scenes that dictates the start of how the next scene starts. There is potential with it, but it will require a lot of balancing of length of tracker and repurcussions of failure as well as when to use it. The one problem I do see with it is that it actively fights boosting and defending as many characters feel that they are on a clock and are playing all out from the beginning in fear that slower strategies will not pay off. I have found the best options is to not introduce the tracker until after the scene has been going for a few rounds and the villains/environment take specific actions to initiate the need for the tracker and impending doom.

 

Initiative: This is a huge concern for me as it actively fights certain play styles, buffs, and overcomes. As noted, there is no benefit to not try and have heroes all go and then villains, the problem is that this can give villains a big advantage in how things play out. For example, if somone uses a boot or buff like galvanize, pick him to start the next round and end his boost early. I find it needlessly random. My favorite solution to this was done in Feng Shui with the shot tracker. Players were put on the track based on a base speed roll and the level of their actions determined how large of a move they made down the track. Act in a lot of small actions or set up one large slow action. It also gives visibility to the order of character turns to set up better combos and strategies.

 

Advancement/Progression: I feel something needs to be done here. I love storytelling, but I feel there still needs to be a carrot to entice players to pursue buildign their character. This happens in comics too as charcters build new tech, learn a new way to use their powers, and discover crazy artefacts. My current thoughts are to introduce permanent buffs in the way of items that can be destroyed as major twists: such as Absolute Zero installing new focus points on his suit to give him a persisten +1 to cold rolls but that can be damaged or even destroyed as a major twist or sacrifice. This at least makes players feel that their character has the opportunity to evolve or advance in meaningful ways but cannot become overpowered over time.

 

My thoughts.

So here's my opinion on RPG's in general: The rules matter.

If you want a game that's basically freeform storytelling, you can use FATE, you can use Amber, you can use No Thank You, Evil.  Or you could use D&D, or GURPS, or anything else you want.  It doesn't make any difference.  You don't need rules that stand up to any scrutiny whatsoever.  You don't even really need a game system at all.  You're just talking and telling stories, and the GM handles things however they feel like.

What would make you choose one game system over another is how they handle mechanically.  And then you get into what rules are balanced, consistent, and smooth.  When one character is more effective than another (when it comes to mechanical/dice-based challenges), that's a strike against the system.  When a player can approach similar problems in similar ways and make similar rolls, yet get drastically different results, that's a strike.  When the rules are hard to remember, hard to execute, or require frequent lookups on a table or flipping through a book, that's a strike.

A good GM can work around even the worst rules to create a fun game.  Sometimes that involves making house rules, sometimes it's filling in ambiguities, sometimes it's avoiding parts of the system that are the most janky.  Christopher did that himself; Sir Arton had an ability that was unfun, so he stopped using it.  That's an example of having a positive experience in spite of the system rather than because of it.  Same thing with initiative: early on, Christopher was expecting a crunchier gameplay style, so when the players screwed up the initiative order, he punished them by advancing the scene tracker 4 spots at at once.  The more sessions that passed, the more he realized that they were never going to be careful tacticians, and the less he punished them for bad decisions.

The reason I bring up Overcome checks as a concern is because of how they're integrated into combat scenes.  In most other games I've played, noncombat abilities can be used or not used in a much more freeform way.  Need to find a piece of information?  You could roll dice to get the word on the street or hit up a library, but you could also role-play talking to people, or pay someone off, or use magic.  When you're in a timed scene and you see a checkbox with "rescue the civilians" next to it, you look at your Principles, Powers, and Qualities, and think up a way to justify applying them to a single roll.  It's not a bad approach, but to me it makes every challenge feel the same.  You also have the "when all you have is a hammer, every problem looks like a nail" thing going on.
