I tried to run my first session last night.  It actually didn't go very well.  Partly this was because there were distractions, but partially because the adventure format is so freeform that both I and my players had a rough time figuring out what to do.

Each issue starts off with "setting examples."  Okay, that's all very well and good to suggest ideas for when a GM is developing their own adventure, but this one is prepublished.  I don't want a laundry list of far too many ideas to introduce at once.  I don't want to throw newbies into a situation where they're asked to improvise almost completely open-ended.  That works really well for some people--the creative, spontaneous sorts--but not for people who like structure.

Since this group of people wasn't up to thinking of their own ways of being heroic without some kind of stimulus to respond to, I tried to warm them up with a building threatening to collapse on top of a refugee camp.  Eyes dive to the character sheets (even though these players know their heroes' archetypes intimately through the card game, and I prompted them ahead of time to think of actions first, character sheet second).

Tachyon wants to do something science-y to figure out why the building is collapsing.  Success with a minor twist: I tell her that they've set up their kitchen area near an exposed girder, which is giving way due to exposure to heat and moisture.  I can't think of a good twist, but then again I'm not sure how much good this information does them.  Lamely, I suggest that fixing the problem would probably destroy their supplies.  Unity does great, magically comandeering an excavator to help prop the building up and buy them more time.  Wraith is completely at a loss, since her skill set doesn't directly apply; she opts to hover around the area looking for anything out of place.  Another success with a minor twist: I tell her there are some men hanging around suspiciously, neither moving to help or escape.  I have some vague idea that they'll seem to be looters but are actually good guys for some reason.

Round two: Tachyon is still struggling with the whole science thing and coming up blank.  Eventually she realizes she can just use her speed to evacuate people, and does so successfully.  Unity animates a slab of ruined metal to act as a gurney and hauls people out (the only roll they made that didn't justify a Principle, but she rolled very well).  The building collapses.  Wraith keeps an eye on the suspicious men and rolls a 10 on doing so stealthily, so I describe some comic panels in which they're looting before the dust even clears, but then Wraith emerges dramatically from the dust and takes them all out before they can react.  (I figure the 10 can make up for the minor twist I never got around to applying.)

And that's as far as we got.  Since it took so long to get through so little, I'm going ahead and reading the other issues for my own entertainment (as we probably won't end up playing them).  There's still a lot I find vague, so I'm trying to line up all the possible gaps as I go.  (Also, my players don't think in superhero terms--their first question was where their characters were living, since their HQ was destroyed and using up scarce hotel rooms or staying in refugee camps didn't seem appropriate for them.)

So, questions so far:

  • Harvester Spider Bots can't reach heroes to attack them if said heroes are flying.  But the spiders shoot laser beams from their eyes, so why not?
  • "Avoiding Collateral Damage" in issue 2 introduces a new concept: twists that get dramatically more severe as the scene tracker progresses.  Nothing in the core rules suggest this should happen.  I see some later scenes apply the same concept.  This seems unbalanced--the GM would be acting strongly in the players' favor if they used scene twists when in the green and "general" twists while in the red, and vice-versa.  I'm not sure how to choose twists in a way that's fair, consistent, and interesting.
  • There's basically no information on the poor family that lives in this house.  I assume the GM makes it up as needed, but I don't know why they should--this info could easily have been in the book.
  • The VoidHeart has one special ability it can use instead of a basic action: create a dark hero and simultaneously advance the scene tracker.  Under "tactics," however, the book says that an Overcome can be used to disable "one of the VoidHeart's special actions".  Does this disable one aspect of its one special action (the manifestation or the advancement) or is that a mistake?
  • The epilogue to issue 2 doesn't suggest how the Visionary knows about what's going on in Insula Primalis, the Tomb of Anubis, or Russia.  "She's psychic" seems very hand-wavy (which, fine) but doesn't help adjudicate what information she might have about each situation for when the players inevitably ask questions.
  • In Prime Wardens #67, Insula Primalis tends to Hinder heroes until its own next turn.  Is this hinder used up on the first roll each hero makes, or is it effectively persistent until it expires or is Overcome?

That's as far as I've read so far.  I'm sure I'll have more questions.

And going into the final issue:

  • The introductions to issues 3-5 suggest that the heroes should be able to identify Baron Blade as the mastermind behind all these disconnected adventures.  However, I don't see any way they can accomplish this.  They're supposed to be able to deduce that "Only a brilliant yet twisted inventor could create all the robots and inventions the heroes have faced."  And then they're supposed to jump to the conclusion that only Baron Blade fits the profile, despite seemingly being dead.  They're even supposed to think that, if Blade is in fact dead, this is the work of a "copycat," even though nothing in any of these adventures fits anything Blade has done historically.  If I were a player coming into this who legitimately thought Blade was dead (ignoring the comic book tropes about the dead not staying dead), I'd figure it was some new opponent, probably some villain from another reality (which Arataki's presence and backstory would seem to hint at, as well as Miss Information's reality-twisting powers and Fanatic's new black sword) or some dramatic transformation of an existing character (like Anubis, Proletariat, and to a lesser extent Fanatic).  Unity also might suspect RevoCorp if her player is the suspicious type.
  • However, failing to figure out that it's Baron Blade doesn't seem to have any specific consequences.  There are references to it having consequences, but none are ever spelled out or even suggested.  He "gets the drop on them," and his escaping is the "most likely result," but I don't see how or why.
  • Scene 1 provides only one option for locating the underwater base: examining a robot to figure out where its signal is coming from.  If the heroes don't include Tachyon or Unity, both the players and the GM will have to come up with something extremely creative to move this forward.  And if they don't, the whole adventure shuts down.  This is, frankly, extremely sloppy design, especially given that this is to be played with preconstructed characters with known capabilities.
  • (Sidenote: this flaw is present throughout the whole packet.  The writers could have very easily provided hooks or suggestions throughout that specifically apply to the six heroes involved, instead of leaving it so open-ended.  Future products won't have that advantage, but this is an intro, meant to be easier to both play and run.)
  • The module also assumes that the heroes figured out that the spider-bots were being controlled through a signal that used to be in the mothership.  However, it was entirely possible to clear that challenge without ever doing so.  Once again, this leads to a dead-end potential.
  • Then there's this: "The heroes are able to find a basic underwater vehicle for getting there, or spend their hero point bonuses to make something more elaborate."  What?  Nothing in the rules suggests that hero points can be used for anything other than floating bonuses to rolls.  I have no idea what mechanic this is supposed to refer to.
  • Dolphins aren't porpoises, and they swim in pods, not flocks.  And jellyfish come in blooms or swarms.
  • The Scene 3 Threats are just dirty.  The GM adds a threat, for free, every time the number of enemy targets drops below the number of heroes.  So they don't know it, but spending their actions fighting minions is literally self-defeating.  That sucks.
  • Those minions refer to "calling back" to previous issues.  Does that just mean "hey, I remember these" and getting a bonus for doing so?  That's a strange mechanic, and it's vital for dealing with the skeletons.  Also, I'm not sure how knowing what complete skeletons look like would help damage the incomplete ones (especially since the finished ones didn't self-repair, so these are clearly different) or what's actually involved in downgrading the soldiers.
  • What the heck is the "psychic conduit?" What does it look like, what does it do, how can it be disabled, and what does it have to do with the trip to the Void?
  • Has anyone tried fighting Baron Blade yet?  With 70 HP and a ton of defensive abilities, he looks exceptionally hard to take down before the scene tracker runs out, especially if they chose the sneaky approach and thus likely won't even reach him for a few rounds.  Although, again, there's little compensation for the number of players, so a team of 6 will have a massive advantage over a team of 3.  And a team of 2, which is technically allowed, is just... forget it.

@much0gust0

I dunno, as someone who is an RPer, I find MindWanderer's approach to not be the one me or my own past RP groups would have taken to some of these things.

The main one is that they seem to be taking a "everything must be beaten up" approach to everything, when that's not only not the only possible approach, but is very explicitly stated as not being the correct approach in many spots in these books.

They also seem to be taking an approach that the GM must do everything a specific way (and particularly one that must seek to defeat the heroes), when a GM not only can do basically whatever they want ("Rule 0", as it's often known in RP culture parlance), but it's explicitly stated in various parts of the books that the GM is here to help provide an interesting story and not to defeat the heroes, and that the GM should be flexible and also encourage the players to also make things up.

So... I feel some of their critiques are understandable and some feel like, to admittedly be a bit blunt, they didn't actually read the books.

The previous issues have a number of clues scattered throughout that the various tech the heroes encounter is based somehow on Blade tech, nor would I ignore a very common comic book trope in an RPG explicitly based on comic book tropes.

The last issue calls out on page 5 that a consequence of Baron Blade seeing them arrive is that one box from the next scene tracker is ticked off before it starts. The implication is that this also happens if they don't figure out beforehand that it's Baron Blade; the flaw is more the text not making this specific.

Likewise, a lot of the complaints about "open-endedness" in various spots are specifically addressed in the various books with blurbs saying it's OK to be flexible and let the GM or players make up whatever sounds coolest.

For instance, regards the "only Tachyon or Unity can do this or else the story shuts down", the text already explicitly says on page 2 in "Figuring out where to go next":

The proposed elements of Scene I provide a straightforward method for doing so, but if your players want to take a different approach, run with it! Encourage the players to think back over what they already know, leveraging that information to lead them to the robot source.

Some of this is also solved by GM assistance (since again it's explicitly stated the GM's goal in this system is to help narrate and not to defeat the players). If you have a group that isn't already the "I take 20 on Search" type, for instance, then just gently nudge them into being more curious by adding on scene narrations that call out certain aspects as being puzzling. ("The spiders all stop when the Mothership dies, as if invisible strings had been cut. Hmm.") Or if the players are not figuring out on their own that something is the wrong approach, again, it can be called out, so there's no reason for the players to "don't know it". ("You defeat yet another minion but it feels like there's just always more of them no matter how many you take down.")

Likewise, the GM text calls out that hero bonuses can be used to make a more elaborate vehicle so the GM can pass on to the players, "If you use up some of your hero bonuses, you can do some general kitting out of the method you use to get to the underwater base to improve how the next scene goes for you."

Likewise again, the gameplay guide explicitly states that yes, the entire point of writing down what "issues" and "collections" you played through is so you can "call back" to them in future stories.

The psychic conduit has to do with how in Issue #2 Visionary explicitly used psychic mind powers to send everyone into the Void (which another thing the GM should be conveying in narrations). If they don't recall that, then the answer is "whatever way the players come up with that sounds most plausible to their hero's powers", which again is explicitly called out throughout the books as being how players should handle Overcome checks.

Finally, with Baron Blade, GMs are generally expected in most RP systems to scale down or up enemy threats as is needed to match player capabilities including how many players there are. The text could be more explicit in pointing that out, but the GM always has that option to take.

I'm honestly sorry if the tone of this seems a little harsh, but some of MindWanderer's criticisms are already addressed by simply reading the text, some are addressed by the game being obvious you're not supposed to solve everything by beating it up, and some are addressed by the game expecting the GM to do their job and narrate information to the players as appropriate/needed.

much0gust0:

Pardon my ignorance (seriously), but if there really is so much of "The GM does what they want," then why not just have a short couple of paragraphs to describe a story and that's all? What exactly is critical information in the booklet that ISN'T just from the top of MindWanderer, et al's heads?

I wouldn't say it's that there's "so much" of the GM/players doing what they want, it's more that generally in any RP system the GM always has the option to do what they want if the text as written isn't working out for their group, and also that Sentinels seems to be more on the freeform side versus some other RP systems.

The advantage of stuff like this Starter Kit is that any premade adventure provides an extensive framework of plot outlines, obstacles, and enemy stats to work from, which leaves the GM to only have to handle more minor narrative details. Just this system seems to encourage more flexibility than some other RP systems with how you overcome and deal all of those things.

Like to pick one example other RP system and one example from this game, in D&D it'd be far more explicit with some things like "In order to pinpoint the VoidHeart, the players have to figure out how to do these specific steps each with this specific DC to roll against," while here the system is like "You have to pinpoint the  VoidHeart: Overcome" as a thing that needs to be done, and otherwise leaves it freeform in that the GM/players can think up whatever sounds best to their tastes/heroes that will accomplish that end so long as they roll an Overcome check for it.

The advantage to the GM here is more that they don't have to think up the entire overall plot for which this is one thing that must be done.

much0gust0:

Adjacent question, is that the way most RPGs are or do they go into more specifics?

It's hard to say whether most RPGs do this, since I'm honestly unaware of how many RPGs on the market are "freeform" versus "crunch".

So basically, a lot of RPG systems are "crunchy", meaning they rely more heavily on statistics. If this was a D&D game, say, I'd agree with virtually all of MindWanderer's criticisms, because D&D is designed to revolve more heavily on having precise numbers and steps for everything for players to roll against, so it would be a failure on the part of the Kit to leave so much up to the players/GM.

But there's also a lot of RPG systems that are "freeform", where everything is narrative/story driven and adjucation is done by extremely simple and sparse decision mechanics, if not left wholly up to the fiat of the players and/or GM. By that metric Sentinels has far more crunch going on, but is less specific on the story details themselves.

Really I therefore think what Sentinels doesn't do like most systems is that it's a middle-ground hybrid, whereas most systems lean far more heavily towards one or the other. It has more things left open than the average crunchy system, but has far more structure to decision adjucation than the average freeform system. That may be why it throws people off if I had to guess; crunch players have to get used to more of what happens being left up to them, and freeform players have to get used to more stats and dice rolling. I'd think freeform players would have the easier time adjusting to this system, though.

At the risk of jumping into the bonfire here, I'm going to toss in a few observations, coming from the perspective of a fellow RPG designer.

I have no issue with Proletariat having stats in the game.  In truth, I think that Haka, Tempest, Anubis, Visionary and Fanatic should all have stat blocks similar to that of Proletariat, to better delineate their mechanical abilities as NPCs.  And, given that, I think they should have been statted up as just that:  as NPCs.  NPCs don't, and shouldn't, follow the exact same format as PCs in this system, so the inclusion of their stats can and should absolutely look different than their stats as PCs in the forthcoming corebook.

There are, absolutely, points within the adventures that could stand to be more specific in the myriad approaches that players could take to succeed in a given scene.  Stats for Haka, for instance, would come in awfully handy, should the group decide to wrestle her into submission, rather than simply trying to talk her down.  Same with Fanatic in the nuclear plant.  Why couldn't Bunker simply "External Combustion" his way through the legion of Proletariats, burning them in one giant conflagration of clones, clearing the way?  While morbid, that's just a single example of a way--outside of the scenario as written--to succeed in that given encounter.  However, the text necessary to understand that Proletariat is a roadblock, not an adversary, isn't called out or made specifically noted. 

A sidebar here would have done a lot of good:  "Attacking Proletariat:  Your players, upon seeing an old foe like Proletariat, might immediately leap to the attack.  If they do, feel free to enter combat--we've included stats for Proletariat above, if you need them.  However, the mass of clones here doesn't actively attack any Hero unless they've been attacked, and really serve as more of an environmental hazard than an active enemy.  If your players do fight the clones, be sure to narrate the effects of both their attacks--which create more clones--and the fact that the other clones don't even seem to recognize they're being attacked.  Your players may, at that point, decide upon a route other than violence."  That's a single paragraph, but it could go a long way towards the insinuation that, "Hey, there's a better way to go than knives and guns here..."  But, as I'll get into below, space is at a premium...

There are, absolutely, points within the adventures that are 'railroady' in terms of their solution.  I think about the 'chase' through the Egyptian tomb/maze, which is abstracted to the point where there's an entire thread already devoted to how to run that specific scenario, much of which comes down to "handwave it all".  The end confrontation with Blade suffers from timing issues, given the constraints of the GYRO scene tracker mechanic.  

But--and this in no way is meant to be an apology for GtG or Critical Hits--we are looking at an incomplete rules-set here.  Any quickstart is exactly that; incomplete, basic rules.  It may well be--and probably is, truth be told--that the rules for "how to scale encounters, based on player number" is in the GMing chapter of the corebook.  It's not exactly the sort of thing that you, as a writer or a layout guru, try to cram into the 24 page booklet that's going in your quickstart.  It's not essential information to playing the system or running the game; yes, it's useful, but it's not critical.  It gets left out.  Same goes for things like, "how do I design a montage scene?" or "how do I structure this chase throughout the city?"  What we've been given here is a glimpse of what the game is like, a few iconic characters to examine, and a few scenarios within which to play.  No more, no less.

So when MindWanderer notes that--and I'm totally paraphrasing here, so don't come at me with misquotations--there's not enough detail here and we don't have an idea of how to manipulate these encounters to get around the railroaded authorial intent, he's right.  But he also needs to be patient, because that level of detail isn't possible in the type of product currently released.  That sort of granular information has to come in a heftier book.  To me, that argument is like cracking open the Basic Red Box D&D and asking "Well, what if I want to grapple this guy?  Where are the rules for disarm?  This is broken..."  As to the argument surrounding "There's stats there; that implies that we should fight"--that's a false association; only the GM can even see those stats.  To the players, Proletariat is as the GM presents him:  a viable foe, a mindless nuisence, a mass of writhing rubble, or somewhere in between.  Saying that the mere presence of a stat block implies him as an adversary insinuates that the GM literally isn't reading the module, only fishing for stats, which should rarely if ever be the case.

However, with Jeysie retorts with--again, totally paraphrasing; no hate towards either of you at all--well, you just need to interpret the rules as a GM and be creative...he's right.  But, with so many players reaching this RPG through the card game--some of whom have already mentioned limited to no playing or GMing experience--the new players have a right to want guidelines for HOW to be flexible and what they can offer a player:  what makes a good Twist or complication, what goes into a dynamic encounter, and what should go in a stat block.  While many of those details cannot--by necessity--go in a quickstart guide, guidance should be found here, especially if this is to be viewed as an intro-level piece.  That said, the insinuation that "What, aren't you creative enough to think on your own?" certainly hasn't endeared many to your viewpoint, despite the fact that I actually agree with nearly everything you've actively argued as a positive within the text.

 

TL;DR -- Jeysie and MindWanderer, you're both right, but you're coming at each side in a manner that accentuates the axact same fault.  Yes, there should have been more detail about how to GM in a narrative style, but that's not the sort of thing you can really put in a 24 page quickstart guide; I have no doubts that it's going to be covered much more deeply in the corebook, not that it helps now.  Yes, there are certain encounters that were dealt with in a railroad-y or an unwieldy manner; likely, that comes from the fact that with a limited page count, you can only cover so many contingencies.  No plan survives contact with the players; that's the first rule of GMing.  

Honestly, it sounds like the argument being made across the board is, "Why isn't X here?".  If every X was in the starter kit, it wouldn't be a starter kit--it'd be the corebook itself.  And yes, for an intro-level product, the starter kit should have included some additional advice on "What if your players do THIS?", but with word count at a premium, something has to go on the chopping block.  When you're trying to introduce a whole new mechanical system, explaing how powers and qualities and the like work, that doesn't always leave enough space for 'fluffy' items like GM advice.  It sucks, but it's a reality of publishing.

There is a key element that Phillippe-Antoine used during that demo that isn't discussed at all in the Starter Kit, and seems to me to be absolutely crucial for the players to understand what exactly they are supposed to do in a given scene.

That element is index cards. Let me explain.

Phillippe-Antoine started the game by describing the devastation in Megalopolis, then asked each of us what we wanted to do to help with the recovery efforts, specifically asking where we were in the city, and encouraging partnerships when it made sense. He would then write down the name of that location on an index card and put it out in front of us on the table. Then when the spider bots attacked, he wrote "Objective: Defeat the Spider Bots" with a check mark box next to it on another index card, and wrote "d6" for each spider bot on each location index card (3 per player). All of this told us players exactly what we needed to do, how many bots there were, and where they were located. Commence battle, where Phillipe-Antoine crossed out a "d6" every time a spider bot in that location was destroyed. Once all the spider bots were destroyed, he checked the box on the Defeat the Spider Bots card. High fives all around.

Next, Unity examined the bots and found the mixture of code. She also got high enough to merit knowing they all worked together via a particular wavelength frequency that was controlling them via a hive mind setup. Interesting information...

Players get the distress call at Akash'Flora from Argent Adept. Off the heroes zoom! Phillipe-Antoine begins laying out more index cards: "Objective: Defeat the Spider Mothership" with its "d10" marked, and telling us how it's laying eggs at a rapid rate, which are turning into harvesters that crawl onto the tree; "Objective: Find the Signal Source" with no boxes, representing the information we learned about the bots and how they are all being controlled from somewhere; "Objective: Stop the Harvesters" with no boxes, and telling us about the vast horde of bots crawling all over the tree and how there are far too many for us to pick off individually; "Objective: Rescue the Human Shields" with the -2 penalty written out, and telling us that they are being used to protect the harvesters, drawing two boxes representing the effort required to rescue some and then all of the civilians; "Objective: Defeat the Armored Spider Bot Snipers" with their "d6" numbers, and telling us how they've found strategic places to web up and offer superior tactical fire on those trying to interfere with the harvesters; "Objective: Defeat the Roaming Spider Bots" with their "d6" numbers, telling us about how there are some wandering bots acting like soldiers; and finally, "Objective: Rescue Argent Adept" adding a check box and writing "Find Argent Adept" next to it, while saying we heroes know Argent Adept is in trouble but have no idea where he is within the chaos that is happening.

As those index cards kept coming out, the enormity of the problem in front of us heroes became clear. Staring at that table top littered with all those objectives made the scene seem overwhelming. Yet one by one, we each took on a challenge and began earning checkmarks on those objectives. Unity managed to hack a spider bot, locate the transmitter on the Mothership, and even had the spider bot transform into a blinking "weak spot" identifying exactly where we needed to hit the Mothership to knock out all the spider bots in one go. As we uncovered new information in the scene, Phillipe-Antoine added check boxes, such as once Argent Adept was found and checked off, a new box was added with "Save him without further injuries" next to it. When that happened, he added the optional check box, "Stabilize him", which added to the tension of the scene as we realized we weren't sure when an objective would be entirely complete.

The index cards served a vital play experience purpose in focusing the attention of the players on what their objectives were, using language that gave a subtle clue as to what strategy would work best. In this aspect, the Starter Kit fails the new gamemaster as well as the experienced one, as I feel this element to be absolutely necessary to the play experience. It communicates a massive amount of information in a very organized fashion, and facilitates the gameplay by giving the players just enough guidance for such an open narrative system.

Just adding an index card that says "Objective: Get past the Proletariats!" or "Objective: Find Fanatic" and slowly revealing the new check boxes one by one as in saving Argent Adept above, is enough to get players into the swing of what they actually need to do. It was this element of the demo that most reminded me of the card game since it so clearly showed what was going on in the story.

Without explaining this index card element, the Starter Kit fumbles in telling a more crunchy or punchy based game group what they need to do or how to keep track of what's going on. The game can be played without this index card element and the solutions could be happened upon more narratively, but I wouldn't recommend it starting out unless you have a group that can handle that much freedom. For a first time experience of the system, with the Starter Kit as the only exposure to the game, then not having this index card element makes the game more challenging to run and play overall, and can lead to a lot of the frustration as expressed by MindWanderer as regards "What if my group just wants to fight everything?" With the index card element there with a check box that says, "Talk down Fanatic from her murderous rage" or something similar, the group will start understanding that punching everything isn't the only answer, and that in turn can help inform your choice of words when filling out the index cards in future encounters. You could use "Talk down" or just use exactly what's listed in the adventure--"Get Fanatic to stop fighting long enough to actually listen to you".

When I start to look at the adventures with the index card element in mind, everything falls into place for me. I get what the creators are trying to accomplish and why the adventures are written the way they are. That this element is not discussed or shown in the Gameplay Guide nor in any of the adventures is puzzling to me as it feels so integral to the experience and how to actually run the adventures. It could be one of those "little details" that is so insidious in the design and playtest phase that it was hiding in plain sight so to speak, and it was just taken for granted that it would be understood without needing to explain it. Or maybe that's how they found to best run the game and figured it was just a gamemaster trick to help run it and did not actually consider it a part of the system. I can't really imagine playing the Starter Kit without it, nor could I imagine a gamemaster designing their own scenes without using it.

So yeah. Index cards. Use 'em. The entire kit cleans up a bunch when you do. :)

Reading over the thread, my general response (which I think echoed some earlier comments) is that:

Sentinels RPG (SRPG) is kinda crunchy, but not really. It requires perhaps more work than D&D. D&D has a stat or rule for practically everything. Want to pick a lock? You got a Lockpicking skill. Anyone who wants to pick a lock either uses Lockpicking or does it unskilled. If your characters wants to pick a lock, they go to the Lockpicking section.The rules tell the GM how a character can pick a lock.

SRPG is a bit more freeform. Someone playing Wraith might decide: "Use Agility + Investigation" because you're trying to Investigate what's on the other side of the lock, and I don't understand the difference between Dexterity and Agility." :) Or "When I selected Agility, I intended it to mean Dexterity, too."

Someone else running Wraith might say "Use Gadgets + Stealth", because you're using a lockpick and you're trying to sneak through the complex by getting past the door.

And someone who is a number cruncher might say, "I'm going to use Throwing Knives + Investigation, because I've got d10s in both and those are my highest dice. Justification? I'm using a throwing knife to pick the lock."

That's not unique: some of the Cortex RPGs do the same thing. But I can see why it's kinda... schizophrenic. It often requires a combination of thinking outside the box/creativity, and number crunching. And sometimes it gets down to personalities and approaches: what if the GM thinks that using a throwing knife to pick a lock is nonsense? What is the person who chose Wraith (or built their own character) because they wanted to pick locks and thought it was a good combo to do so disagrees with the GM? Let's say that both sides honestly believe their points of view: you get some bad feelings.

You also get some players who do try to number crunch/game like that. Or are they? They rarely say that they do, and often they don't think that they are. Or they think that it's all a part of that type of RPG. Or maybe it's how they get their kicks. Externally, how do you tell the difference between the different types? Is there a difference? Is one "better" than the others?

And yes, this kind of thing should theoretically be run up with the GM before character selection/design. But... there's a lot of potential situations, and several players.

How does this all tie in to the discussion? It seems like we have two sides, one taking the Type A GM and the hardcore approach (which isn't unique to D&D, and D&D-ish GMs), and the other taking the Type B GM and the "If you think of it, they will come and I will approve" approach. The starter adventures seem to be written the same way, IMO. Some parts are very "Do what you will" and some parts are "Here are very specific guidelines and rules for dealing with these situations."

Just my two cents.

Having finished Issue 1 in the PBP as Unity, I have some more feedback to add.

Regarding the issue:

It runs pretty smoothly, but the "Horde of Harvesters" is a bit wonky.  First, the players need to realize that unlike all the other spiders, you don't just hit the horde with damage, you use an Overcome to neutralize all of them at once.  It's unclear why they can't just do the same thing to kill all the other spiders without using an area attack that's actually on their character sheets.

Second, the swarm of spider-bots is described as being unstoppable because they're being replaced so quickly, yet the mothership only lays up to 5 new spiders a round.  It doesn't really state in the issue that the mothership is in fact laying way more than that, but the others are going to the tree.  Without that, it's hard to realize that destroying the mothership means that Overcome actions can be used to clear the tree.

Both problems are solved by using index cards with checkboxes for objectives, as described above.  Once again, it really seems that this was the intended method of playing the game despite not being overtly stated in the books.

Regarding Unity:

Unity... has some problems, I feel.  She's fun but she has some design flaws.

  1. She's far too dependent on minions.  I get that it's her whole archetype, but the minions are fragile and it's easy to find yourself without any, either because they were destroyed or because it's a new scene.  At the beginning of an adventure, it's fine, she just makes them.  But when she reaches Yellow status, if she doesn't have any minions, she has to choose between falling back to a Green ability or using Brainstorm.  I ended up using Brainstorm a lot; it's a great move even though it keys off a d6 power.  But if you're using that, you're ignoring Unity's archetype and over half her ability set.  When she reaches Red, she can start making two minions a round, but at that point the scene is almost over and the time to build up your forces is past.  Usually only rounds 7 and 8 are Red, and if she makes minions in round 7 they'll only get one chance to act.
  2. Her Principles are unbalanced.  Every single one of her Powers is technology-related.  Other heroes are more diverse and can approach challenges in different ways.  With Unity it's technology or bust.  This means that she's going to be using Principle of the Gearhead virtually constantly.  Principle of Levity, on the other hand, is extremely challenging to use, and even if she can think of a way to use Levity with one of her Powers, she could probably have used Gearhead too!  If she had Banter or Insight as a Power (several other heroes have Insight as a Power), it would help a ton.
  3. She has both Science and Technology as Qualities.  That seems redundant, and since Technology uses a bigger die, she'll be using that pretty much exclusively.  And again, since all of her Powers are technology-related, using that big d10 Technology die is going to be the "correct" choice nearly every time.  I ended up using Former Intern and Insight just for fun a few times, but I could have easily justified Technology instead every time.
  4. Overcome is a trap choice for minions.  As Dave described, the success table for Overcome is designed with Principles in mind.  Mostly the heroes will be using their Principles when Overcoming, and as a result they'll usually either succeed outright or succeed with a minor twist.  Minions, on the other hand, usually have a d8 to roll at best, meaning the chance of outright success is only 1/8.  They're much more likely to create twists or fail, and you do not want a bunch of minions creating additional twists.  Twists are serious business, even minor ones, and unless those twists are directed solely back at the minion that created them, they can get the whole team into a lot of trouble very quickly.  And if you're using an action to create a minion that acts once, creates a twist, and then either destroys or hinders itself, Unity would have been better off just taking that Overcome action herself.  If you want minions to help with an Overcome situation, give them the Boost action.
