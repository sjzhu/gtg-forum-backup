1) Define a kicker -- When rolling to attack or overcome (not sure about create or defend -- didn't use those) you select three dice. One comes from which of your powers you are using. The second from one of your qualities. The third is based on the maximum of either your person stress level or the scene's stress level. For example, Absolute Zero might get a D10 die for using 'Cold Control', a d8 die for using his quality of 'Shooting', and another d8 die because his personal stress level has reached Red. THEN, he might decide to use one of the 'kickers' that's available to him, such as Thermal Schockwave. Thermal Schockwave isn't available until AZ's or the scene's stress level reaches Red. Thermal Schockwave would then do something like allow him to attack multiple targets, and/or use some die other than just the middle die when determining how well he succeeds.

 

2) Correct: Yellow and Red kickers/statuses (note: this is just a working name for these things) are only available when the character and/or scene have reached those statuses.

 

3) I don't recall an attack or overcome being resisted, but it may be that we simply didn't get to it in our playtest. I do know that it's possible to have kickers/statuses that occur as a reaction to something else. AZ, for instance, uses his Coolant Blast kicker/status whenever he personally changes from one zone (green, yellow, red) to another.

 

4) Modifiers to a die. Not sure how much of this was actually in the rules, but in our demo we were playing that under certain circumstances one of the results of an overcome role could be to add a positive modifier to some future attempt to overcome. The idea was that instead of attempting to overcome something yourself, you're attempting to help someone else overcome that obstacle, so instead of applying the mid roll to the attempt to overcome, you take the _min_ roll and that becomes a positive modifier to some other heroe's future attempt. NOTE: A lot of the rules are still very much in flux!

Our group also had a tendency to keep wanting to do multiple things at once. Our DM allowed us to attempt that, but only by taking various minor and major costs.

 

5) Initiative: It's about the same as 13th age, if you happen to know how that works. Typically, the main villain starts. Before taking their turn, whoever's turn it is decides who will take the next turn. Everyone must act in each round before anyone else can take a turn.

 

6) Goons and villains and their play behavior: The scenario we faced was a bunch of goons attempting to blow up a new wormwhole/gate device that was designed to make transport to Wagner Mars Base easier. The heroes were at the scene for various reasons. The goons, masquerading as scientists, planted charges to destroy the gate. After the charges blew, the minions pulled out ray guns and started firing at the heroes. The GM put down a series of index cards representing our obstacles: Fix the gate, which was pulsing with weird energies; Calm the paniced crowd; Defeat the minions; Put out the huge fire; Stop the robots that were attacking the crowd; Stabilize the pillars/roof that were threatening to collapse.  Only the minions were taking an action, but everything else was still a direct threat that needed to be dealt with. Each minion was represented by a D8. When attacked, the 'effective' die (my adjective) was compared against a D8 roll by the minion. If the minion was equal to or _less_ then the attacking die, it was destroyed. Otherwise, it dropped down to a D6. If a minion was a D4 and would otherwise be dropped down, it was destroyed. On their turn, each minion would attack one of the heroes, rolling their durrent die and causing that much stress.

First off, I absolutely love the dice pool assembly.  The Star Wars RPG works in a very similar way (albeit using their own special dice with its own crazy symbols), and is something I think works extremely well for story based RPG's, especially combined with the "succeed at a major/minor cost" element.

I never played Marvel Heroic, but based on how Christopher laid out the video, it does seem very similar to what you are describing.  As for all decks being equal, I think that is a hard balance to strike in an RPG.  In a board/card game like SotM or Tactics, it's very easy to just make the numbers fit.  Legacy rocket punching a minion group from orbit does the same damage as Mr. Chomps biting on a minions leg?  Sure, why not.  It can be made to fit because a card game and tactical game exist to be balanced first and thematic second.

However, an RPG exists to put yourself into the shoes of that world and those characters, so balancing can be a bit more touchy.  When you have a character like Legacy whose bullet proof, super strong, flying, spidey sense self can solve most problems by blinking an eye, you need to put him in the same universe as The Naturalist who can turn into a Rhino and say "Yep, these two are equally powerful."  That sort of balancing challenge is definitely made possible through narrative story telling and I'm glad the Sentinels RPG seems to be going that route.

The same issue was pressed with Edge of the Empire characters existing in the same room as Jedi characters from Force and Destiny.  How the hell is a smuggler supposed to stand confidently beside a Jedi Knight and say "Yeah, we're basically the same power level."  And the answer for F&D at least is the Morality system.  Whenever you make a key decision as a player, depending on the result, you can be given Conflict.  At the end of the session, you roll a D8 and subtract the conflict from the result, and the net number is the increase/decrease in your Morality.  So basically, yeah, if you're a Jedi and you want to be uber powerful Min Max McAwesomesauce, you can…but… your character is going to go down a dark path very quickly, because I guarantee the GM is going to be putting some difficult decisions in front of you.

I hope to see some similar mechanic in Sentinels (or perhaps one already exists in Marvel, which will be transcribed in some way) as I think that's a great way to give all characters of ridiculously different power levels a sense of balance.  And overall, I'm yapping endlessly about something we have very little confirmation on anyways, so maybe it's just best to be patient.

Some other things I gleaned from the demo and jotted down. I jotted it down in a doc but forgot about it until tonight, so I'm sharing now:

Scenes

three scene feature categories: threats, obstacles, and challenges

Threats hit back. often tied to a villain (as spoken by the GM). Threats were individual dice (d6, d8, etc.). If your attack result > die type, eliminate threat, otherwise step down the die (d8->d6, d6->, d4->defeated). 

unresolved obstacles increase the scene stress, in the example it was one point per obstacle.

challenges take multiple successes to overcome. if they are not resolved by the time the track gets to "out," bad things happen.

 

Scene zone colors & stress

Green: 2 or 3 points

Yellow: points = # obstacles

Red: 2 or 3

Out

Laid out like:

G:OOO | Y: OOOO | R: OOO | Out

Mark 1 circle per obstacle left at the end of the round.

aidanhurst:

Thank you Phil for all the information you've given us. Two questions if I may;

  1. Can you talk a little bit more about how experience will work?

  2. What sort of timeline are you looking at for the beta/getting more playtesters involved and are there any open slots for playtesters?

It's been a pleasure!

  1. As of now (subject to change), experience represents the number of adventures your character has been in since you started playing with it. Each adventure gives you "an issue" in a comic book collection.  During play, you score "spotlight moments", moments where your hero shines through great success, great failures or just plain weird events. There are mechanics that cover how to get those moments.

At the end of an adventure (that can last from one to a few sessions)  all players tally up thier spotlight moments. The character(s) with the most are featured as the main character(s) of that particular issue. And the group names the issue appropriately. (ex: Legacy #52: Peril on Mars) or (Legacy and Unity Special #2: Portal Trouble in Idaho). All other players are considered secondary characters in that issue.

Players mark both types of issues on their character sheets: Spotlight ones and ordinary issues where they were secondary characters. There are mechanics to make it harder for players to get consecutive spotlight issues. 

So XPs are like a comic book collection that tells of your previous adventure.

You tap into them in two ways:

Callbacks: Once per spotlight issue per adventure, you can callback to a previous adventure where your hero did something that would help you in a particular situation. Doing so either counts as minor cost to offset a minor failure, or allows you to Max out one die of your pool after a roll.

Trade Paperbacks: Once you get 6 issues in your collection, regardless if they are spotlight or not, you trade them away and your character "levels up" and gets a dice size increase on a power/quality and other options we're working on. 

I'm real excited about these (as I came up with a few of the ideas behind them, refined by Cam and Dave. amazing designers that they are.)

  1. I can't answer that, because I don't know. I know people can't wait to playtest this, but it's not entirely in our control and much of our energy is spent on designing the bits that are still missing.
