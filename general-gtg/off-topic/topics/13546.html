The Jakobshavn Glacier in south-western Greenland is one of the fastest flowing ice streams in the world (around 20m/day on average) and is responsible for a significant percentage of Greenland's iceberg output into the Atlantic. It is generally considered likely that it produced the iceberg that sank the Titanic, given the position of the Titanic when struck.

For a typical glacier, the calving face (or area where the hundreds-of-meters-thick glacial ice meets the sea) is relatively stationary, with flow toward the face balanced by icebergs calving off into the sea. However, over the course of the past 30 years, the Jakobshavn Glacier's claving face has been retreating rapidly up the fjord following a deep under-ice (and below sea-level) trench in the bedrock. Because the trench increases in depth for the next 25km or so beneath the ice stream, it is thought that the retreat could continue, or even accelerate, over the course of the next few decades.

Hundreds of years ago, most glass windows were typically produced by taking a glob of glass and spinning it really fast until it flattened out into a disc, like tossing pizza dough. Window panes were then cut from the disc, which was thick in the middle and thin around the edges, so the surfaces weren't flat and the windows weren't uniformly thick. This is why many old window panes are thicker at the bottom (if you put the thick side on top, the pane is more likely to break), but some people mistakenly assumed that glass is actually not a solid, but a very viscous fluid, and the glass at the top would slowly flow down to the bottom.

There have been many other methods for manufacturing sheet glass, but the coolest one is a somewhat recent development (probably within the last 100 years). You take a vat of molten tin and pour the molten glass in on top of it. The tin is more dense, so the glass floats and forms a layer on top. Because they are both liquids, the layers are self-leveling; so the glass would have flat, parallel surfaces on top and bottom. Finally, tin has a lower melting temperature than glass, so you can let the glass cool and solidify while the tin stays liquid. The result is a pane of glass with flat, clean surfaces and uniform thickness, so no grinding or similar post-processing is required. I believe this method is used to create most modern window panes.
