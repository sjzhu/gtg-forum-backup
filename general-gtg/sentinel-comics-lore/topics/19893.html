The color coding of heroes and villains and the psychology of color is a whole big ol' thing. There are obvious exceptions and counter-examples to all these ideas, so they're less rules and more generalities, and they hold more true in the silver age than later when people were intentionally messing with the color schemes and playing around with antiheroes.

Bright primary colors (red, yellow, blue) and white are usually heroic. They're colors often associated with flags, which a lot of early heroes were designed to reflect.

Secondary colors (green, purple, and orange) and gray are usually villainous, or at best anti-heroic, and especially if they're darker or unnaturally saturated.

Black can go either way as a base color to make anything added to it more dramatic, but it tilts towards villainy.  Heroes who wear black mostly started out as the outsiders, untrustworthy and dangerous.

And sure, the Hulk is green-purple, but he's also not exactly a heroic character; at best he's a weapon you can point the right direction and hope it doesn't come back your way.

The psycology of color is complicated, but in general humans dislike colors that suggest disease, decay, and injury. A pure bright red is probably okay, but blood-red is rarely a good sign. Dark purples often suggest bruises, while dark greens usually look more like rotting plants than a cheerful meadow.

And yellow is tricky -- while a slightly orangey yellow can have a golden effect (good!), adding even a little green into yellow makes it suggest sickness (it's the color of bile), and darkening it at all pretty rapidly gets into a gross mustardy color that probably doesn't go with a hero. Yellow is very common as an accent color, but very few heroes wear primarily yellow clothes; at best, it's combined with equal amounts of red or blue to balance the outfit.
